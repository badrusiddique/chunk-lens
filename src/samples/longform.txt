Vector databases have become a fundamental component of modern AI applications, particularly those built on retrieval-augmented generation. Unlike relational databases that store and query structured data using SQL, or document stores that index JSON documents, vector databases are optimised for a single primitive operation: finding the k nearest vectors to a query vector in high-dimensional space.

An embedding is a dense numerical representation of a piece of information produced by a neural network. The critical property of a good embedding model is that semantically similar inputs produce geometrically nearby vectors. Two sentences that convey the same meaning should have vectors that are close together in the embedding space, even if they share no words in common.

This geometric proximity is what makes vector search useful. To answer the question "which documents in my corpus are relevant to this query?", you embed the query and search for documents whose embeddings are near the query embedding. The search returns documents ordered by relevance, and those documents are passed to a language model as context.

The practical challenge is scale. A corpus of one million documents, each embedded into a 1536-dimensional vector, requires 1,000,000 times 1,536 times 4 bytes, or roughly 6 GB of raw storage just for the vectors, before any metadata or indices. Exhaustive nearest-neighbour search over such a corpus would require computing one million dot products per query, which is feasible on modern hardware but adds measurable latency for interactive applications.

Approximate nearest-neighbour algorithms sacrifice a small amount of recall accuracy for dramatic improvements in query latency. The most widely deployed algorithm is HNSW, Hierarchical Navigable Small World, which builds a multi-layer graph where each node connects to nearby neighbours at different scales of granularity. Query time is sub-linear: you navigate the graph rather than scanning the entire corpus.

The choice of embedding model matters as much as the choice of retrieval algorithm. An embedding model trained on general text may not capture domain-specific terminology well. A medical corpus benefits from a model fine-tuned on clinical notes; a legal corpus benefits from a model trained on case law. The dimensionality of the embedding also affects storage requirements and retrieval speed. Lower-dimensional embeddings are cheaper to store and search but may lose fidelity on subtle semantic distinctions.

Chunking interacts with all of these factors. If a chunk is too large, its embedding averages over too much content and becomes a fuzzy representation of many topics. If a chunk is too small, it captures a fragment too short to carry a clear meaning. The ideal chunk size depends on the embedding model's context window, the typical query length, and the downstream use case. An embedding model with a 512-token context window cannot meaningfully represent a 2000-token chunk because it will silently truncate the input.

Operational concerns are significant when running vector databases in production. Indexing throughput, memory footprint, shard balancing, and incremental index updates are all more complex than in traditional databases because approximate nearest-neighbour indices are not designed for fine-grained mutation. Some systems require a complete index rebuild when a large fraction of vectors are updated. Others support soft deletes with periodic compaction. Understanding these trade-offs before choosing an infrastructure stack saves significant pain during scaling.

Monitoring a vector search pipeline requires custom metrics that do not exist in traditional database monitoring. Recall at k, the fraction of true top-k nearest neighbours that appear in the returned results, is the primary quality metric, but it requires a ground-truth labelled set to compute. Latency percentiles, memory pressure from the HNSW graph, and index build time are secondary operational metrics. Without these in place, silent quality regressions are easy to miss when the corpus grows or the query distribution shifts.

The embedding model and the chunking strategy together determine retrieval quality. Getting one right while neglecting the other limits the overall system. Systematic experimentation on representative queries, with measurable recall targets, is the only reliable way to know whether the combination works.
